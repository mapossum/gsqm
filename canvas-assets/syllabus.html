Course Description

GHY 417/517 introduces the quantitative methods used to describe spatial and non-spatial geographic data and to test hypotheses about spatial processes. The course moves from the nature of geographic data and descriptive statistics, through probability, sampling, and classical inference, into spatial-specific methods: point pattern analysis, spatial autocorrelation, and spatial regression. Examples draw from environmental geoscience — Gulf coast hurricanes and wetlands, Mississippi River hydrology, southeastern forests, environmental justice in Mississippi communities, and biodiversity data. All work is completed in Python using Google Colab, with publication of results to ArcGIS Online.

The focus throughout is on knowing when to use a technique and what it tells you about a spatial process — not on which buttons to click. Graduate students (517) complete additional reading critiques and an independent research project alongside the standard coursework.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course you will be able to:

  1. Describe a geographic dataset using appropriate non-spatial and spatial descriptive statistics, and explain why a chosen statistic is appropriate for the data's measurement scale.
  2. Apply the framework of statistical inference — sampling distributions, hypothesis tests, p-values, confidence intervals — to environmental questions, and recognize when classical assumptions are violated.
  3. Choose and execute the correct hypothesis test (one-sample, two-sample, ANOVA, chi-square, and non-parametric alternatives) for a stated research question.
  4. Analyze spatial pattern using point-pattern methods (quadrat, nearest-neighbor, K-function) and area-pattern methods (Moran's I, Geary's C, LISA), interpreting results in terms of underlying spatial processes.
  5. Build and interpret correlation and regression models — OLS, multiple regression, and a spatial-regression / GWR variant — and diagnose violations including spatial autocorrelation in residuals.
  6. Communicate statistical and spatial results in plain language and through reproducible Python notebooks and ArcGIS Online web maps or story maps.
  7. Use generative AI tools responsibly: prompt effectively, verify output, identify hallucinated methods or syntax, and cite usage.

Graduate students (517) will additionally be able to:

  1. Critique the methods of published spatial-analysis papers, identifying assumptions, threats to validity, and possible improvements.
  2. Design and execute an independent spatial-statistical analysis using a dataset relevant to their research interests.

Course Structure & Weekly Rhythm

The course runs on a Monday–Wednesday rhythm that repeats every week. Every module opens on the same day of the week, and every deadline falls on the same day of the week — no surprises. Because we are fully online and asynchronous, this predictable rhythm is the scaffold that keeps you on track.

Each module includes

  • A Weekly Overview page with objectives and a checklist.
  • Learning materials: assigned reading from the textbook, a segmented video lecture (4–6 short videos of 6–10 minutes each with in-video knowledge checks), downloadable slides, and a one-page Data Card describing the dataset used in the week's lab.
  • A short reading quiz to confirm you engaged with the reading.
  • A guided lab notebook (Colab / Jupyter) that walks you through the week's method on real data, with cells for you to complete and interpret. All labs are solo work.
  • A weekly discussion where you find an outside example of the week's method applied to an environmental question.
  • An AI Workflow + Verify section inside the lab notebook (see AI Policy).

Deadline rhythm (every week the same)

Day What is due
Monday 8 am Module opens (or Tuesday 8 am if Monday is a university holiday). Start-of-week announcement sent.
Friday 11:59 pm CT Discussion original post due.
Monday 11:59 pm CT (of the following week) Reading quiz due. Hard close; two lowest quiz scores dropped.

Deadlines never fall on a Saturday, Sunday, or university holiday. Because the lab is due the following Wednesday, you have 8–9 days per lab. This is intentional — modules overlap by design, so you can preview the coming week while you finish the current one.

Reading 1.5h · Segmented video 1h · Quiz 0.5h · Lab notebook 3h · Discussion post + replies 0.5h · Capstone work 2.5h · Slack and review 1h. Total ≈ 10 hours per week.

Generative AI Policy (USM Option 1: Encouraged Use)

Generative AI tools (Gemini in Colab, ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, and similar) are encouraged in this course. Statistics and GIS are exactly the kind of domain where AI tools accelerate learning when used well and mislead you when used poorly. Your job in this course is to learn both the methods and the judgment to use AI responsibly with them.

You may use AI for

  • Explaining concepts in different words.
  • Debugging code.
  • Suggesting which test to use.
  • Generating code you then adapt and/or verify.
  • Drafting written interpretations that you then verify and edit.
  • Learning Python library syntax.

You must

  1. Verify every method, formula, or test recommendation against the textbook or another authoritative source. AI tools sometimes invent Python spatial-stats syntax that does not exist.
  2. Include an AI Usage Statement at the end of every lab and the capstone: which tools you used, what you asked and why.
  3. Submit work that you can explain and defend if asked.

You may not

  • Paste a lab prompt into an AI tool and submit its output without verifying it.

Every lab notebook contains a short AI Workflow cell — a specific prompted exploration tied to that week's method — and a Verify & Reflect cell where you explain what AI got right or wrong. These normalize the "use AI, then audit AI" loop.
