Week 1 — Reading Guide

Assigned Reading

  1. Lembo, McGrew & Monroe (2024), Chapter 1 — "Statistical Techniques and Geography." Read the whole chapter (~20 pages).
  2. Lembo, McGrew & Monroe (2024), Chapter 2, first section only — "Geographic Data: Individuals and Attributes." Just the opening pages that introduce the vocabulary. We'll finish Chapter 2 next week.
  3. AI Literacy Primer (below, ~10 minutes) — a short piece I wrote for this course.
  4. (Optional but recommended) USGS Earth Science Data Fact Sheet — a quick primer on how federal agencies talk about environmental data. Link in Canvas.

Total reading: about 1.5 hours.

What to Look For in Chapter 1

Chapter 1 is the framing chapter for the whole course. Rather than memorize anything, come away with clear answers to these questions:

  • What is a "statistic" in the technical sense, and how is it different from a "parameter"?
  • What is the distinction between descriptive and inferential statistics? Which one is Chapter 3 going to be about, and which one is the second half of the course about?
  • The authors highlight several types of geographic problems that quantitative methods help with. Which ones jumped out at you as relevant to environmental questions?
  • Why is spatial data treated specially in the book (and this course)? What are one or two things that make a spatial dataset harder to analyze than a non-spatial one?
  • The concept of spatial autocorrelation appears briefly in this chapter. Note the definition — we come back to it as the central concept of weeks 11 and 12.

What to Look For in Chapter 2 (first section only)

Just get comfortable with the vocabulary:

  • What is an observational unit (also called an "individual" in the text)?
  • What is an attribute or variable? How does the text distinguish variables that describe the location of an individual from variables that describe some characteristic of the individual?
  • Why does the author distinguish spatial attributes from aspatial attributes?

You do not need to master measurement scales this week — we do that carefully in Week 2.

Note-Taking Suggestion

Keep a running "concept map" in a notes doc as you read. For Chapter 1, three or four boxes with arrows between them (population → sample → statistic → inference back to population) will serve you well when we get to Weeks 4 and 5.

AI Literacy Primer for This Course

This short piece is required reading for Week 1. It sets the culture around AI use in GHY 417/517.

Generative AI tools — Gemini in Colab, ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, and others — are extraordinary tutors for a course like this one, and they are extraordinary ways to bypass learning if used carelessly. This course actively embraces the first use and calls out the second. Here is how to think about it.

AI is a fluent tutor with a lying problem. Large language models are trained to produce text that sounds correct. They are not trained to produce text that is correct. When you ask an AI "what Python function computes Moran's I in PySAL?" you will get an answer that sounds authoritative. It may or may not be the correct answer. It may reference a function that does not exist. This is not a bug you can prompt your way out of — it is a fundamental limitation of the technology. Your job is to enjoy the fluency while auditing the correctness.

A useful mental model. Treat every AI response the way you would treat advice from a very well-read friend who has never actually done the thing. Their explanation will be clear; their code may or may not run; their citation may or may not exist. That's fine — you still learn a lot from the well-read friend, but you never trust their code without running it or their citation without checking it.

What this looks like in practice for this course. In every lab notebook you'll find an "AI Workflow" cell with a specific prompt tied to that week's method, and a "Verify & Reflect" cell right after it. The Workflow cell asks you to use an AI tool. The Verify & Reflect cell asks you to say, in your own words, what the AI got right and what it got wrong. Over the semester, the pattern of "use, then audit" becomes automatic. That habit is the durable skill.

Where AI is genuinely helpful in this course.

  • Debugging a Python error message you don't understand.
  • Explaining a statistical concept in a different way when the textbook's explanation didn't click.
  • Drafting boilerplate code (loading a file, formatting a plot) so you can spend your time on the analysis.
  • Reviewing your written interpretation and suggesting ways to make it clearer.
  • Answering "what test should I use for X situation?" as a starting point, which you then verify against the textbook.

Where AI creates problems for your learning.

  • Pasting the whole lab prompt and submitting the output. You skip the learning entirely, and I can tell.
  • Asking AI to write your discussion post. Peer reviewers can tell; I can tell; you cheat yourself out of practicing scientific writing.
  • Trusting AI-generated statistics code without running it and comparing to the textbook's example. AI often invents plausible-sounding function names.
  • Using AI on quizzes or exams. Prohibited unless a question explicitly says otherwise.

What you must do. Every lab notebook and the capstone project ends with an AI Usage Statement. Three to six sentences, in plain English: which tools you used, what you asked, what you accepted, what you rejected, and why. This is not a punishment — it is metacognition. Writing it forces you to notice what AI actually helped with and where it wasted your time. Students who write good usage statements consistently outperform students who don't, because they are building the audit habit.

Fabricating an AI Usage Statement counts as academic misconduct in this course. Not disclosing AI use is also misconduct. Disclosing it is easy and never gets you in trouble.

A closing thought. Statistics is the discipline of reasoning carefully in the presence of uncertainty. Generative AI is an especially interesting subject for statistical thinking, because AI outputs are themselves uncertain — sometimes right, sometimes wrong, in ways that follow patterns you can learn to recognize. This course is one place where you get to practice that kind of reasoning on the technology as well as on the data.
